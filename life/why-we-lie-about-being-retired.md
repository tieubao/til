## Context

Summary of a BBC article exploring the psychological dimensions of retirement, based on research by Harvard Business School professor Teresa Amabile and perspectives from Nobel laureates. Source: bbc.com/news/business-48882195.

## Retirement as identity crisis

Over four years, Amabile's team interviewed 120 professionals about retirement. The finding: after the initial relaxation wears off, many retirees face an existential crisis. Planning for retirement is treated as a purely financial exercise, but it needs to be a psychological and relationship exercise as well.

Retirees cling to professional identity. They introduce themselves as "retired librarian" or "retired research chemist." Some deny being retired entirely, because they don't want to be seen as "yesterday's news."

## Work as meaning

Nobel laureate Edmund Phelps (economics, 2006, mid-80s): "Work is the main source of a meaningful existence for most people." He sees denying older people their place in the "wonderland of work" as wrong, especially where mandatory retirement ages exist.

Novelist Mario Vargas Llosa works at his desk seven days a week: "I do not have the feeling that it's a job. Writing is a pleasure for me."

## The inequality problem

Rising retirement ages affect people differently. Knowledge workers can extend their careers far more easily than those doing physical labor. Harvard economist David Bloom warns this inequality could generate serious friction, comparing potential "silver riots" to the aspirational frustrations behind the Arab Spring.

The policy challenge: raising pension ages serves white-collar workers while leaving blue-collar workers behind. This inequity has already produced unrest in parts of Europe, and it could get worse.

## Key takeaway

Retirement planning that only considers finances misses the harder questions: Who will you be when your career ends? And can society afford to treat retirement as a one-size-fits-all milestone when the nature of work varies so dramatically?
