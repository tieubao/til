## Context

In 1970, a Zambia-based nun named Sister Mary Jucunda wrote to Dr. Ernst Stuhlinger at NASA's Marshall Space Flight Center, asking how he could suggest spending billions on a Mars mission when children were starving. His reply, published by NASA as "Why Explore Space?", is one of the most eloquent defenses of scientific investment ever written.

## The parable of the count and the microscope

Stuhlinger opens with a story. A medieval German count gave generously to plague-stricken townspeople, but also funded a man grinding lenses in his laboratory. The townspeople were angry at the "waste." But that work led to the microscope, which did more to eliminate plague than any amount of direct charity could have.

The lesson: investing in research with no immediate application can yield returns that dwarf direct aid.

## The economic argument

The space program consumed only 1.6% of the US national budget (about $30 per taxpayer earning $10,000/year). Stuhlinger explains that government budgets are line-item specific. If NASA's budget were cut, those funds would simply not be collected from taxpayers; they wouldn't automatically redirect to hunger relief. A separate budget line item would need congressional approval for that.

## How space exploration fights hunger directly

**Food production**: Earth-observing satellites can screen wide areas for watershed control, fertilizer use, weather forecasting, crop surveys, and harvest planning. Even a modest satellite system was estimated to increase global crop yields by billions of dollars equivalent.

**Food distribution**: The real barrier is international cooperation, not shipping capacity. Space programs foster cooperation between nations. Stuhlinger cites Apollo 13: the Soviet Union discontinued radio transmissions to avoid interference and positioned rescue ships, despite Cold War tensions.

## The catalyst principle

Significant technical progress often comes not from direct approaches but from setting audaciously challenging goals that fire the imagination. Space exploration is exactly this kind of catalyst. About a thousand technical innovations per year flow from the space program into everyday technology: kitchen appliances, farm equipment, medical instruments, communications, weather forecasting.

The same applies to scientific knowledge. The space program attracts young scientists, funds research, and provides extraordinary natural phenomena to study.

## The Earthrise photograph

Stuhlinger enclosed the famous "Earthrise" photo from Apollo 8 (Christmas 1968). He calls it perhaps the most important result of the space program: it showed Earth as "a beautiful and most precious island in an unlimited void," awakening awareness of environmental limits and the urgency of addressing pollution, hunger, poverty, and overpopulation.

## The core argument

> "How much human suffering can be avoided if nations, instead of competing with their bomb-dropping fleets of airplanes and rockets, compete with their moon-travelling space ships! This competition is full of promise for brilliant victories, but it leaves no room for the bitter fate of the vanquished, which breeds nothing but revenge and new wars."

Space exploration takes over a function that for thousands of years has been the "sad prerogative of wars": driving technological development and scientific discovery, but without destruction.

Stuhlinger closes with Albert Schweitzer's words: "I am looking at the future with concern, but with good hope."
