## Context

A blog post aimed at software engineers about spending time on things that matter and avoiding things that don't. The core thesis: time is the only real currency, and engineers waste it on low-impact activities while neglecting high-leverage practices.

## Things that matter (time well spent)

**Typing speed and tool mastery.** Fast typing reduces friction between thinking and implementation. More importantly, mastering your IDE (keyboard shortcuts for refactoring, debugging, navigation) compounds over months. Ten minutes daily learning shortcuts yields 5+ hours annually. Terminal tools like Vim, Tmux, and tiling window managers eliminate context-switching costs.

**Automation with team-wide impact.** The real ROI calculation: if one person spends 3 days automating a 30-second task for 10 engineers, payback occurs within 6 months. The "5X engineer" myth is usually someone who helps the whole team save time, not someone who codes faster individually.

**Infrastructure knowledge.** Understanding deployment mechanisms, containerization, and datastore fundamentals lets you diagnose issues systematically rather than guessing. Learning MySQL configurations and query optimization prevents cascading failures that eat everyone's time.

## Things that don't matter (time sinks)

**Language and technology favoritism.** Programming languages cannot be objectively ranked. Successful companies use Java, Kotlin, Perl, Lisp, and C++ interchangeably. Focus on what works for your context, not ideological purity.

**Premature scale concerns.** Most applications never approach their scale limits. Modern hardware handles surprising volume. Good data modeling solves future scalability better than adopting distributed systems prematurely. Benchmark before switching.

**Shiny new tool syndrome.** Don't adopt emerging technologies to demonstrate knowledge. Evaluate whether a solution addresses an actual problem in your current context, not a hypothetical future need.

**Criticizing others' code.** Context changes over time. Legacy code reflected constraints when it was written. Respect peers, understand historical decisions before judging, and accept that no perfect code exists.

## Key takeaway

The article frames time management not as a productivity hack but as a value system. Every hour spent on technology tribalism or premature optimization is an hour not spent on automation that helps your team, infrastructure knowledge that prevents outages, or mastering tools you already use. The multiplier effect matters more than individual speed.
