## Context

Mark Seemann (ploeh) argues that "passion" in software development is more useful to employers than to developers. The word itself comes from the Latin for "suffering," which turns out to be fitting. This note distills his argument about why treating passion as a weakness rather than an asset protects your career.

## The passion trap

The software community developed a culture where passion is the expected default. You blog, speak at meetups, contribute to open source, build side projects. Employers love this because passionate people work for free and are easier to manipulate with praise. Open-source maintainers build critical infrastructure that companies profit from, receiving only recognition in return, until they burn out.

The implicit deal: show a stunning portfolio of free work to get hired. Unlike artists, you have already given away everything in that portfolio. Employers benefit; you worked for free.

## The self-improvement bind

Many employers refuse to invest in employee training. The classic fear: "If we train them, they will leave." The result is that keeping your skills current becomes entirely your responsibility. If you do not, your skill set slowly becomes obsolete, your marketability atrophies, and your employer can treat you progressively worse because you have nowhere else to go.

This is not about passion. It is about self-preservation. You must invest in your own growth whether or not your employer supports it.

## The privilege caveat

Seemann acknowledges his own path is not reproducible. He started blogging when it was novel, answered Stack Overflow questions when the site was new, and lives in Denmark with free healthcare and generous social security. The early movers in tech evangelism occupied niches that no longer exist. Modeling your career after these outliers is risky.

## The dispassionate stance

Caring about your craft is fine. The problem is when passion becomes the justification for working without compensation, for accepting poor treatment, or for letting employers extract free labor under the banner of "culture fit."

The recommendation: regard passion as a vulnerability to manage, not a virtue to maximize. Keep learning, keep building skills, but do it for self-preservation, not for applause. Be dispassionate.
