## Context

Dave Cheney, a Go conference organizer and frequent speaker, shares advice on writing conference proposals. The key insight is that a proposal and a presentation are different things targeting different audiences - the proposal targets reviewers, not the eventual audience.

**Source:** [How to write a successful conference proposal](https://dave.cheney.net/2017/02/12/how-to-write-a-successful-conference-proposal)

## The core distinction

Your talk and your proposal target different audiences. The talk is for the stage audience; the proposal is a pitch to reviewers. Writing a good proposal is a separate skill from writing a good presentation.

## Focus on the audience

Start by identifying the audience. Consider not just skill level (beginner, advanced, expert) but the kind of people at the conference. A vendor conference audience (managers, sales, decision makers) wants to hear about reliability and adoption, while software engineers care about performance and extensibility.

## What reviewers want

Reviewers are charged with recommending talks they think are interesting and appropriate. Most reviews are confidential and anonymous in initial rounds. Reviewers judge your proposal and your ability to present it using only the submission form fields. They are looking for talks people will pay to see.

## The three key fields

**Title:** Your one-line elevator pitch. Keep it clear rather than clickbaity.

**Abstract:** Usually printed in the program. One sentence on the topic, one sentence on what the audience takes away. That is all you need.

**Talk description:** This is where proposals succeed or fail. You need to accomplish three things:

1. Make it clear you know what you are talking about. Cite your experience or sources.
2. Show you have a plan to communicate within the time limit. Include an outline of your talk headings or bullet points.
3. Address all the selection criteria for the conference. If none are published, ask the organizers.

## The critical rule

Proposals with more detail beat proposals with less detail. Reviewers cannot take it on faith that you will do a good job. The less you write, the more risk they see.

However, do not overload either. A proposal covering every possible aspect of a subject signals you cannot manage your time.

## Common mistake: selling snow to Eskimos

Do not sell the audience on something they already like. At a JVM conference, do not pitch "the JVM is great." Instead, show a project that was only possible because of the JVM. Let your work demonstrate the value rather than preaching it.
