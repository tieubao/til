## Context

Charlie Munger's 2007 USC Law School commencement address, distilled into a personal operating system for life. From Farnam Street. Munger opened with: "No doubt many of you are wondering why the speaker is so old. The answer is obvious: He hasn't died yet."

## The principles

### Deserve what you want
The golden rule: deliver to the world what you would buy if you were on the other end. People with this ethos win not just money and honors, but the deserved trust of the people they deal with.

### Admire the right people, living or dead
Admiration-based love is the highest form. Include the instructive dead in your circle of admiration.

### Lifetime learning is a moral duty
You are not going to get very far based on what you already know. "If civilization can progress only when it invents the method of invention, you can progress only when you learn the method of learning."

### Master multidisciplinary thinking
The big ideas carry 95% of the freight. Pick up the big ideas from all disciplines and make them a standard part of your mental routines. Practice or you lose it. Build a mental latticework that you use automatically for the rest of your life.

Warning: this works so well that you will frequently know more than experts in their own specialty.

### Think backwards
Inversion frequently solves problems nothing else will. Instead of "how can I help India?", ask "what is doing the worst damage in India, and how do I avoid it?" They sound logically the same but they are not.

### Be reliable
Unreliability cancels out all other virtues. Doing what you have faithfully engaged to do should be automatic.

### Avoid intense ideologies
Ideology cabbages up the mind. When you announce loyalty and shout orthodox ideology, you are pounding it in and ruining your mind. Munger's iron prescription: "I'm not entitled to have an opinion on this subject unless I can state the arguments against my position better than the people supporting it."

### Eliminate self-serving bias, envy, resentment, self-pity
Self-pity gets close to paranoia and is one of the hardest things to reverse. Munger's friend carried index cards that read: "Your story has touched my heart. Never have I heard of anyone with as many misfortunes as you." Every time you drift into self-pity, give yourself one of those cards.

At the same time, allow for self-serving bias in others who have not removed it. Ben Franklin: "If you want to persuade, appeal to interest, not to reason."

### Avoid perverse incentives
Incentives are too powerful a controller of human cognition and behavior. Billable hour quotas at law firms are an example of a system that creates perverse outcomes.

### Work under people you admire
Maneuver cleverly, without criticizing anybody, so you work entirely under people you admire. Your outcome in life will be way more satisfactory.

### Maintain objectivity, especially when it is hardest
Darwin paid special attention to disconfirming evidence, particularly when it disconfirmed something he believed and loved. Use checklist routines. No other procedure works as well.

### Concentrate power in the right people
Get practice into the hands of people with the most aptitude to learn. You do not want your brain surgeon chosen by taking turns.

### Follow your intense interests
You can force yourself to be fairly good at many things, but you cannot be really good in anything where you do not have an intense interest.

### Assiduity: sit down and do it until it is done
Two partners had one rule: "Whenever we're behind in our commitments, we will both work 14 hours a day until we're caught up." They died rich.

### Use setbacks constructively
Epictetus: every mischance in life is an opportunity to behave well and learn something. Your duty is not to be submerged in self-pity but to utilize the terrible blow in a constructive fashion.

### Build seamless trust
The highest reach of civilization is a seamless web of deserved trust. Not much procedure, just totally reliable people correctly trusting one another. That is how an operating room works at the Mayo Clinic. "If your proposed marriage contract has 47 pages, my suggestion is do not enter."
