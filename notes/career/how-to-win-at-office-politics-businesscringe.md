## TL;DR

Corporate advancement is not decided by competence, output, or tenure. It is decided by the invisible scoreboard in the boss's head, which tracks perception, not performance. The video argues you cannot opt out of this game because it runs whether you participate or not, and the side that plays wins by default. It catalogs three manipulation tactics people already use against you (association hijacking, strategic mirroring, reputation erosion) and three defensive plays you should adopt (build alliances above your pay grade, own your narrative, be a strategic yes-man). The whole thing is framed with a comedic cynicism but the underlying claims map cleanly to the real organizational-behavior literature.

## Main topics

1. **The iceberg model of a workplace.** The official layer (mission statements, JDs, performance reviews, "we reward hard work") is a thin surface. The real workplace underneath runs on perception and boss-feelings, not on output.
2. **The invisible scoreboard.** Every employee has a mental score in their manager's head. It tracks "do I like being around this person" and "does this person make me feel smart/safe", not results.
3. **Offensive tactics (what others use on you).**
   - Association hijacking
   - Strategic mirroring (chameleon effect)
   - Reputation erosion (gossip behind the back)
4. **Defensive/offensive tactics (what you should do).**
   - Build alliances above your pay grade
   - Own your narrative, always
   - Be a yes-man strategically (pick your battles)
5. **The opt-out trap.** Refusing to play is itself a move, and it loses. Comparison: showing up to a competitive game saying "let's just have fun" while the other team is playing to win.

## Key nuggets

- **Perception beats performance.** "The scoreboard isn't tracking your output, your skills, or your results. It's tracking how your boss feels about you." The promotion decision happens in the 30 seconds after you leave a meeting, not in your performance review.
- **The chameleon effect is real.** Humans unconsciously mimic people they trust. The inverse also works: if you deliberately mimic (speech cadence, laugh timing, values language), people trust you more. This is a documented psychological phenomenon, not just advice.
- **12% of corporate leaders show psychopathic traits.** Video cites this as one-in-eight leaders. The point isn't "bosses are evil", it's that charm, manipulation, and narrative control are systemically rewarded by corporate selection, so people wired for those traits rise faster.
- **Association hijacking is usually not theft.** The move isn't stealing someone's work. It's being present at the right moment, saying one thing that gets quoted back, and letting the boss's memory do the rest. The work itself stays credited; the *aura* of the work gets transferred.
- **Networking ≠ alliance building.** Networking is LinkedIn and business cards. Alliance building is identifying specific people with power over your outcomes and making sure your name comes up favorably in rooms you're not in.
- **You can't sit out.** The shadow workplace runs whether you engage or not. Not playing is a choice to lose.
- **Credit does not find you on its own.** "You don't ever wait around hoping credit magically finds you, it won't."

## Nuances and counterpoints

- **The cynical framing is a feature and a bug.** The video is deliberately dark/comedic to make the point stick, but real workplaces are a mix. Pure performance *does* matter at small companies, early stages, technical depth roles, and companies with functioning review systems. The scoreboard model is most accurate in large bureaucracies with weak performance signals. Calibrate the dose.
- **"Strategic yes-man" has a ceiling.** If you always agree, you become invisible and untrustworthy on hard calls. The actual pattern from organizational research: disagree on things that show you're thinking, comply on things where your preference genuinely doesn't matter. The video flattens this into "just shut up" which is wrong at senior levels.
- **Reputation erosion is high-variance and mostly negative-sum.** The video lumps it in with the other tactics, but it is different in kind. It's a tactic with legal and cultural blast radius (HR, retention, sabotage). People who use it habitually tend to flame out once they hit a ceiling of people who can see through them. Treat this as a tactic to *detect and defend against*, not to deploy.
- **The iceberg model is a frame, not a rule.** Some workplaces genuinely do track output, especially in roles with hard measurable signals (sales quotas, shipped code, billable hours). The perception game still exists but sits on top of the measurable work, not in place of it.
- **"Charm is selected for" ≠ "you should be charming."** The 12% psychopathy stat is descriptive of who gets promoted in broken systems, not prescriptive advice. The actionable version is: *be aware that systems reward these traits, so expect to compete against people wired this way, and build defenses.*
- **What the video doesn't say: the actual best play is to be talented AND politically aware.** The highest-leverage path isn't pure politics (you burn out or get caught) or pure competence (you get steamrolled). It's building rare, visible skill and *also* running the perception layer. The video skips this because it's less entertaining.

## My takeaways / how to apply

I'm on the other side of the table as founder of Dwarves, so most of this video flips on its head for me. But the frame is still useful in several ways:

- **I am a scoreboard-holder for ~15+ people.** The video is an uncomfortable mirror: my mental model of each contractor is doing the same thing. Worth checking whether my scoreboard is tracking signal (actual delivery, judgment, client feedback) or noise (who Slacks me back fast, who speaks up in syncs). Probably some of both, which is a problem I should fix by making the evaluation rubric more explicit.
- **Client-side politics is the real game for a consultancy.** Inside Dwarves the "office politics" stakes are low. But the client's internal politics determines whether contracts renew, budgets survive, and champions defend us in their own org. Treating our primary client contact as someone whose scoreboard we need to keep healthy (make them look good to *their* boss) is probably the single highest-leverage lesson from this.

The video's "alliance building above your pay grade" maps directly to how we should think about account coverage: don't rely on one champion, have quiet coverage across multiple stakeholders.

## Open questions

- Is there rigorous research behind the "12% psychopathy in corporate leaders" stat, or is it a pop-psych number? (Checking: this likely refers to Babiak & Hare 2010, which actually found ~3-4% in a specific sample of corporate managers. The 12% figure may be inflated.)
- What's the honest breakdown of "how much of promotion is perception vs. output" across company sizes? This probably varies by 10x between a 10-person startup and a 100k-person enterprise.
- How do remote/distributed teams change the calculus? Strategic mirroring and reputation erosion both rely on informal face-time. Does Slack/Zoom shift the equilibrium toward output-visibility, or does it just move the politics into different channels (DMs, emoji reactions, who gets @ mentioned)?
- Where does the line sit between "own your narrative" (healthy) and "take credit that isn't yours" (association hijacking)? The video treats one as defense and the other as offense, but the actual behavior is on a continuum.

## Transcript

Winning in the corporate world has nothing to do with performance, skills, or years of experience. None of that garbage matters. No. What it all comes down to is office politics.

> Office politics? Like, who runs for office president?

No. Office politics is when some bro got promoted over you even though he's a gazillion times less competent than you. Why? Because he laughs at the boss's dry jokes. He raises his hand during all-hands meetings to ask pointless questions, making himself appear invested. He's somehow become friends with all the important people. That's office politics. It's that invisible social game at work fueled by power dynamics, norms, and conformity. Trash like that.

And the problem is you can't opt out of this game. This game is corporate life. It's the very foundation of it. If you don't play the game right, you're out. So here's how to play it.

### The iceberg model

When you first start working at Big Corp, you're young and naive. You have no idea how the corporate world actually works, but it's not what it seems at first. It's kind of like those iceberg graphs you've probably seen. At the top, you have the visible stuff, the official stuff. You've got your mission statements, job descriptions, the performance reviews, the town hall meetings where your boss tells you, "We're in this together." That official part of your workplace says it rewards competence and hard work. That's the deal on paper.

But if you look at the big chunk underneath the visible part, you find the real workplace. The one nobody puts on a slide.

Okay, but what actually is that? Well, in short, it's about perception, not performance.

### The invisible scoreboard

Picture this. Everybody at work is on a scoreboard, an invisible scoreboard that exists in the mind of your boss. That scoreboard isn't tracking your output, your skills, or your results. Of course not. It's tracking how your boss feels about you. Is he impressed by you? Can he be around you without losing his mind? Are you in his eyes a bit of an idiot or a total idiot?

This is the game of office politics and it's always running in break rooms, during elevator rides, and in the 30 seconds after someone leaves a meeting. That's when the real game is played.

And listen, you can't sit out office politics. If you want to get anywhere, you have to participate.

> There ain't no getting around it. Period.

Studies show that up to 12% of corporate leaders have traits associated with psychopathy. 12%. That's one in eight. Why? Because psychopaths are people wired to run on charm, manipulation, and narrative control. And it actually serves them well. Make of that what you will.

### Offensive tactics others use

**Tactic one: Association hijacking.** Picture this. You've worked your ass off on a project for 3 weeks. You present it to your boss, hoping he'll give you some well-deserved cred.

> So that's why our Q3 sales are projected to increase modestly but noticeably. Thank you for listening. This presentation was my life's work.
> Really good, Mickey. Really good.
> Yeah, I think we should really focus on setting this in motion quickly.
> Quickly. That's very true. Sharp thinking, man. That thing he said about setting this in motion quickly. That really stuck with me. That guy is so sharp. When promotions come around, I'm keeping him in mind.

That's association hijacking for you. It's not necessarily about stealing your work. It's just about opening your damn mouth at the right moment and associating yourself with something that was already successful. This guy's score on the scoreboard just went up.

**Tactic two: Strategic mirroring.** This is a real psychological phenomenon rooted in something called the chameleon effect. It's the natural human tendency to unconsciously mimic the behavior and mannerisms of people we interact with. Everyone does it to some degree. It's biology. We trust people who think like us, talk like us, share our values, and people who look like us.

So strategic mirroring at the workplace looks like this. If you act like your boss, talk like him, walk like him, laugh at his jokes, he'll subconsciously view you as a better person. He'll like you more.

> Hey guys, check out the shirt. Hilarious right?
> That is hilarious, boss. You're so funny. Your sense of humor correlates with mine to a very significant extent.
> So, who should we promote?
> I don't know what it is about Jessica. She just gets it, you know? I like that girl.

**Tactic three: Reputation erosion.** This one is the nastiest and the sneakiest, because unlike the other two, this one isn't about making yourself look good. It's about making someone else look bad. Slowly kill their reputation behind their back. It works like this.

> Sorry, I got to run. Good chat, guys. You know, I saw Mickey put some of those buns in his backpack yesterday. I'm not accusing him of anything, but it kind of sounded like he wanted us to not want them so he could have them for himself. I'm not implying anything, just putting it out there.

So, it's gossip. Reputation erosion is a fancy way of saying you talk about people behind their backs, like immature kids. But hey, it works, doesn't it?

### Defensive plays you should run

**Step one: Build alliances, especially above your pay grade.** I'm not talking about networking. That's lame. Networking is for LinkedIn influencers and people who collect business cards for fun. That's not this. This is about going into the war zone, that is your workplace, and identifying people you could have usage of and becoming besties with them. At least have them think well of you. Get on their right side.

Best practice here is finding people above your level, above your pay grade. Folks who actually know what you do and think well of you. The goal is to make it so that when your name comes up in a room you're not in, someone else has got your back.

> Hey Larry, you know what I've been thinking? I am absolutely dying to hear more about that stamp collection of yours.

Building alliances like this sucks. It really does. But it gives you a little bit of a protective shield.

> Someone washed everyone's favorite t-shirt with dark clothes. It is ruined. I want to know who did this right now.
> I don't recall.
> I don't recall.
> I don't recall.

Build alliances, people.

**Tactic two: Own your narrative.** This is about always having a story to back your stuff up. It's about not leaving anything up for chance. If you finish a project, you make damn sure you're the one presenting it to leadership. If you're in a meeting, make damn sure to raise your hand and repeat whatever nonsense your boss said to make it seem like you contributed. You don't ever wait around hoping credit magically finds you, it won't.

And if anyone ever questions you, why were you late? Why your name isn't on the Q3 report? Why you weren't in that meeting? You always have an answer.

> Mickey, your name isn't anywhere in the Q3 report. Were you even involved in that project?
> Oh, the reason my name was not in the Q3 report was because I'm already working on the Q3 report 2 years from now. In fact, those other brothers are the ones who should step up.
> That is the damn nicest thing I've heard anyone say since I graduated from sixth grade.

Have a story. Own your narrative.

**Tactic three: Be a yes man, strategically.** There is a time and a place to have opinions. Your salary negotiation? Yes. Voting on a new office coffee maker? Yes. But when it's something that could really rub your boss the wrong way, you shut that trap up.

> Hey, Mickey. Do these jeans look good on me?
> They look great, boss.
> Yeah. You're sure they're not women's jeans?
> I've never been more certain of anything in my life, sir.

Get on their right side. I know it sucks, but just swallow your pride and do it.

### The opt-out trap

Sadly, you cannot opt out of office politics. It's not possible. It's not a choice you get to make. The shadow workplace exists whether you engage with it or not.

If you decide not to play it, it's kind of like rolling up to a sports game and going, "The goal of today's game is to make everyone feel good. It's all about having fun." But the other team is going, "We must win at all cost. Crush them."

The other team would obviously destroy you, and that's why bad people get promoted. If you don't play office politics, you end up like Bill. You know, doing the work of 30 people and being paid for a third of a normal person's salary.

Office politics is what's up whether you like it or not. So play it. Play it to win.
