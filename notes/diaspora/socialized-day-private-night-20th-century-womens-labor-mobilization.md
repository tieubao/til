Many 20th-century states tried to free women from household and childcare labor to mobilize them into the industrial workforce. The intuition that "they all took the kibbutz approach and succeeded" is half right and conceals the most important finding.

## The question and the correction

Two different things get conflated:
1. Mobilizing women into work via **daytime** collective childcare and communal services. Many states did this and largely succeeded as labor policy.
2. The kibbutz's **residential** model, where children slept apart from parents. Almost no state replicated this at scale.

The USSR, China, and Vietnam built daycare, canteens, and factory-kitchens, but children still went home to sleep. Full residential separation was close to unique to the kibbutz. So women were mobilized into industry on a huge scale, but not the kibbutz way. They did the moshav-adjacent thing: socialize the daytime layer, leave the family's nighttime bond intact.

## Five-model comparison

| Dimension | Kibbutz (Israel) | Soviet Union | Maoist China | Socialist Vietnam | Japan |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Driving goal | Build the "new Jew", equality | Industrialize, dissolve bourgeois family | Mobilize total labor, "hold up half the sky" | War mobilization, then Doi Moi growth | Rebuild via male-breadwinner model |
| Childcare mechanism | Residential children's house | Daytime creches, kindergartens | Daytime creches, Great Leap canteens | Cooperative creches, Women's Union | Minimal; mother stays home |
| Kids slept at home? | No (the radical exception) | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Communal dining | Yes, central | Factory-kitchens (under-resourced) | Great Leap canteens (collapsed ~1961) | Limited | No |
| Female labor outcome | Very high among members | High, sustained | Very high, then declined post-Mao | Very high (~70% today, top in SE Asia) | M-curve: drops at childbirth |
| Double burden? | Less so (truly socialized) | Yes, severe | Yes, severe | Yes, documented | Avoided by keeping women home (different cost) |
| Persisted? | No, reversed by 1990s | Eroded under Stalin, gone by 1991 | Reversed after Mao | Participation persisted, ideology faded | Slowly reversing toward dual-earner |

## The dividing line

![Socialized day, private night](https://assets.han-ws.workers.dev/i/2026/05/collectivism-socialized-day-private-night.svg)

Four of the five drew the line in the same place: socialize the day, keep the night private. Only the kibbutz crossed into the night, and that is exactly the part that collapsed first. The state socialist models, despite rhetoric about the family "withering away," never actually pried children out of the home to sleep. They could not afford it, and where they ideologically wanted it, ordinary families resisted.

## Country reads

**Soviet Union.** Loudest anti-family ideology (Engels/Lenin, family to "wither away," domestic work as "domestic slavery"). Built factory-kitchens, communal dining, creches, all aimed at the daytime layer. But resources never matched the rhetoric (a contemporary study estimated ~7 million domestic workers needed per 20 million people). When canteen food was bad or unaffordable, families ate at home and the woman's domestic role reinforced. Under Stalin the state reversed: the 1936 and 1944 Family Codes retreated from emancipation, because the Party concluded the traditional family running on unpaid female labor was the cheapest childcare.

**Maoist China.** Largest version. "Women hold up half the sky"; the Great Leap mobilized women en masse, including Iron Girls doing traditionally male heavy work; canteens briefly socialized cooking (collapsed with the famine ~1961). Recent scholarship: the mobilization was driven by state labor needs more than genuine gender equality, and coexisted with reinforced traditional roles (women got the factory shift and kept the home shift). After Mao, explicit reversal (Iron Girls mocked, women nudged back to female roles). Female participation stayed high by world standards but has declined (~64% in 2010 to ~61% in 2019).

**Socialist Vietnam.** Mobilization origin was the war (it cut the prime-age male population, so when Doi Moi growth began in 1986 the labor supply was predominantly female). Women's Union and cooperative creches handled the daytime layer. Unlike China, high participation persisted: ~69% today vs ~51% world average, ~77% for the prime 25-49 group, highest in Southeast Asia. The footnote: Vietnam's own statistics office is blunt about a disproportionate double burden and persistent inequality in unpaid family labor. A traditional saying holds women to "good at national tasks and good at housework," the double burden compressed into a proverb.

**Japan (the pattern-breaker).** Did the opposite. Postwar high growth entrenched the male corporate-breadwinner / female professional-housewife model, backed by the tax system. The signature is the M-curve: participation peaks before marriage, drops during child-rearing, returns later at a lower peak in irregular low-wage part-time work. Japan industrialized to the top of the world economy while keeping women home during the child-rearing years. The cost was not the double burden; it was paid in one of the world's lowest fertility rates and childcare centers too short-houred for both parents to work full-time. Japan proves the mobilization was not necessary for industrialization. You can get rich without it; you just pay a different bill.

## Verdict

Three conclusions. First, the mobilization was real and largely succeeded as labor policy, but it was a daytime operation; the "freeing from children's-household attachment" was real only in the kibbutz, and that is precisely the piece that failed. Second, the universal hidden cost was the double burden: none of the socialist states actually socialized domestic labor, they added wage labor on top of it, because the unpaid family was the cheapest childcare and no state wanted to fully replace it. Third, the durable equilibrium everywhere is some version of *socialized day, private night*, which is structurally the moshav, not the kibbutz.

## Bridge-builder connection

Across five societies and a century of experiments, the daytime cultural-commons layer is yours to build richly, but the transmission of identity runs through the private parent-child channel, and history is unusually consistent that this channel resists being socialized away.

## Honest caveat

This compares across wildly different scales (a few hundred kibbutzim vs nation-states) and the older Soviet/Maoist figures are soft. The direction of the finding is robust and repeated; the exact participation numbers are not. Treat the pattern as the strong claim and check any single figure's year and source before citing it.
