## Context

Distilled from a Business Insider article drawing on Walter Isaacson's biography of Steve Jobs. These are the persuasion and negotiation patterns Jobs used repeatedly to push Apple and Pixar forward.

## Pitch with passion

People respond to strong displays of emotion. Wynton Marsalis said Jobs talked for two hours straight about iTunes: "He was a man possessed. After a while, I started looking at him and not the computer, because I was so fascinated with his passion." Jobs infused the same intensity into ad campaigns, producing iconic spots like the "1984" commercial and iPod silhouette ads.

## Be brutally honest

When Jobs returned to Apple in 1997, he told employees: "The products suck! There's no sex in them anymore!" People bought into his ideas because he was always earnest. His rule for the room: "You've got to be able to be super honest."

## Earn respect through work ethic

After returning to Apple while still running Pixar, Jobs worked 7 a.m. to 9 p.m. daily, suffering kidney stones in the process. Consistent presence and relentless effort earned him the respect that made his demands effective.

## Disarm with seduction and flattery

Jobs could charm anyone at will. People like Amelio and Sculley believed his charm meant respect. It was strategic: he could be charming to people he hated and insulting to people he liked.

## Claim good ideas as your own

If your position was better than his, Jobs wouldn't just acknowledge it. He'd adopt it as his own. Bud Tribble: "If you tell him a new idea, he'll usually tell you it's stupid. But exactly one week later, he'll come back and propose your idea as if he thought of it." The Genius Bar concept followed exactly this pattern.

## Decide quickly

Jobs rarely relied on studies or surveys. When Jony Ive proposed candy-colored iMacs, "in most places that decision would have taken months. Steve did it in a half hour." Quick decisions sometimes meant being wrong (the CD tray vs slot drive debate), but the speed meant shipping on time and iterating fast.

## Fix problems immediately

"If something isn't right, you can't just ignore it and say you'll fix it later. That's what other companies do." Jobs applied this at Pixar (rescuing Woody's character from Disney's edits) and at the Apple Store (delaying launch 3-4 months to reorganize by customer activity rather than product type).

## Handle problematic people decisively

Jobs saw the world in binary: hero or bozo, amazing or garbage. When a mouse engineer said "no way to build such a mouse commercially," Jobs fired him. The replacement's first words: "I can build the mouse."

Alternatively, for situations requiring patience: follow the "line of least involvement." Jobs would go silent and ignore uncomfortable situations, letting time and neglect resolve them.

## Strike when the iron's hot

After Toy Story's success, Jobs took Pixar public despite bankers and even Lasseter saying to wait. The IPO exceeded Netscape as the biggest of 1995, freed Pixar from Disney dependence, and ultimately led to Disney acquiring Pixar for $7.4 billion, making Jobs Disney's largest shareholder.

## Use leverage ruthlessly

On his first Thursday back at Apple, Jobs demanded stock option repricing and threatened to leave if the board didn't comply. Then he demanded all board members resign except Ed Woolard. By choosing his own board, he gained the independence to greenlight products like the iPod.

## Demand perfection

Jobs found adequacy "morally appalling." He badgered engineers over boot times, demanded any iPod function be reachable in three button presses, and insisted on halving production timelines. Larry Ellison: "Steve created the only lifestyle brand in the tech industry."
