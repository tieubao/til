## Context

An argument from Graham Lee on why calls for discipline in software engineering consistently fail to produce industry-wide change. The problem is not that discipline is wrong, but that it has no built-in motivation to spread.

**Source:** [Discipline doesn't scale](https://www.sicpers.info/2020/10/discipline-doesnt-scale/)

## The argument

When you ask people to slow down and think harder about what they are doing, the responses split into groups:

- **Some fraction will comply.** They hear the message and change their behavior.
- **Some will look for alternatives.** They want the benefit without the cost and may find a shortcut.
- **Some will not pay attention at all.** They were never listening.
- **The dangerous ones** thought they were paying attention but end up not doing the disciplined thing. They either torpedo the idea entirely or transform it into something unrecognizable (see: OOP, Agile, Functional Programming as cultural movements that drifted far from their origins).

The vast majority just were not listening and you will never reach them.

## Why it fails

There is no strong motivation to become more disciplined. A developer can use their current tools, satisfy their customer, and get paid. Learning the "right way" slows them down, and the payoff is unclear. Who benefits? The person teaching? So the teacher can feel validated? That is not a compelling value proposition.

## The implication

If you want to improve engineering practices at scale, relying on discipline and education alone will not work. You need to change the environment: better defaults, better tools, systems that make the right thing the easy thing. Discipline is a personal virtue, not an organizational strategy.
