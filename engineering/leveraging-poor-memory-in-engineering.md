## Context

This article argues that having a poor memory can be a superpower in software engineering. Rather than memorizing APIs and syntax, engineers who cannot rely on memory develop compensating habits that make them more effective.

**Source:** [Chasing 10X: Leveraging A Poor Memory In Engineering](https://senrigan.io/blog/chasing-10x-leveraging-a-poor-memory-in-software-engineering) (original site defunct)
**Attachment:** [Chasing 10X: Leveraging A Poor Memory In Engineering.pdf](https://github.com/tieubao/til/files/3026456/Chasing.10X.Leveraging.A.Poor.Memory.In.Engineering.pdf)

## Core argument

Engineers with poor memory develop three compensating strategies that actually produce better outcomes:

**1. Writing things down obsessively.** When you cannot trust your memory, you document everything - decisions, rationale, gotchas, architecture. This creates a knowledge base that benefits the entire team, not just yourself.

**2. Building systems instead of relying on recall.** Rather than memorizing deployment steps or configuration details, poor-memory engineers automate and systematize. Scripts, checklists, CI/CD pipelines, and infrastructure-as-code all emerge from the inability to remember manual steps reliably.

**3. Writing simpler, more readable code.** If you cannot hold complex abstractions in your head, you write code that is self-documenting and straightforward. You avoid clever tricks because you know you will not remember what they do in three months.

## Why this matters

The "10x engineer" myth often celebrates people who hold entire systems in their heads. But this creates bus-factor risk and knowledge silos. The engineer who cannot remember everything but builds great documentation, automation, and simple code creates leverage that scales across the team.

## Actionable takeaways

1. Document decisions as you make them, not after
2. Automate any process you have done more than twice
3. Write code for the version of you that has forgotten everything about this project
4. Build external systems (wikis, runbooks, tests) instead of relying on tribal knowledge
