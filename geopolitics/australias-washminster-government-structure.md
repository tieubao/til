## Overview

Australia is a federal parliamentary constitutional monarchy, often called the "Washminster" system because it blends the British Westminster parliamentary model with US-style federalism and a written constitution. The constitution was adopted in 1900 and took effect in 1901 when six British colonies federated into a single country.

![Australia's government structure](assets/geopolitics/australia-government-structure.svg)

## Two axes

The system is best understood along two dimensions:

**Axis 1: Separation of powers (horizontal)**
- Legislative: Bicameral Parliament with Senate (76 seats, 12 per state, 2 per territory) and House of Representatives (150 seats). Both houses have nearly equal power; consent of both is needed for legislation.
- Executive: Prime Minister and Cabinet. The PM is head of government by convention, appointed by the Governor-General. Ministers must be members of Parliament.
- Judicial: High Court with 7 justices, serving as the final court of appeal and arbiter of constitutional disputes.

**Axis 2: Federalism (vertical)**
- Federal (Commonwealth): defence, foreign affairs, immigration, trade, taxation, post
- States (6) + Territories (2): health, education, justice, police, internal transport, land management. Each state has its own constitution, Governor, Premier, and parliament.
- Local government: ~537 councils handling roads, waste, planning, parks, libraries

## Components

| Component | Details |
|-----------|---------|
| Head of state | King Charles III (ceremonial), represented by Governor-General |
| Head of government | Prime Minister (Anthony Albanese, Labor, since 2022) |
| Upper house | Senate: 76 seats, proportional representation (STV), often not controlled by government |
| Lower house | House of Representatives: 150 seats, full-preferential voting, 3-year max terms |
| High Court | 7 justices, appointed by Governor-General on PM's advice |
| States | NSW, VIC, QLD, WA, SA, TAS (each with own bicameral parliament, except QLD which is unicameral since 1921) |
| Territories | ACT, Northern Territory (self-governing, 2 senators each) |

## Key design decisions

**Powerful Senate.** Unlike the UK House of Lords, Australia's Senate is a genuine legislative check. It uses proportional representation, so the government rarely controls it and must negotiate with minor parties and crossbenchers.

**Hard constitutional amendment.** Changes require majorities in both houses PLUS a referendum winning both a national majority and a majority in at least 4 of 6 states ("double majority"). This is why the constitution has barely changed since 1901.

**Ceremonial monarchy.** The sovereign's functions are almost entirely formal. A 1999 republic referendum was defeated, but the movement remains active.

**Residual powers to states.** Unlike the US where the Constitution enumerates federal powers and reserves the rest to states, Australia's constitution does the same thing but in practice the federal government has expanded its reach significantly through the corporations power and tied grants (conditional funding to states).

## Why this matters for the Hormuz crisis

The federal/state split explains why the fuel crisis response requires coordination. Fuel excise and strategic reserves are federal. Emergency services, transport infrastructure, and service station allocation are state responsibilities. This is why Albanese convened "National Cabinet" (a coordinating body between federal and state leaders) rather than issuing executive orders unilaterally.
