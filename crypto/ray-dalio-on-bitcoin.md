## Context

Ray Dalio's personal assessment of Bitcoin, published in early 2021. He frames himself as a "non-expert" but provides a structured analysis of Bitcoin's potential as a store of value. The piece includes a Bridgewater research appendix by Rebecca Patterson et al. examining Bitcoin's viability as an institutional storehold of wealth.

## Dalio's thesis

Bitcoin is "one hell of an invention" - a new type of money programmed into a computer that has worked for ~10 years and is rapidly gaining popularity. Like the credit-based monetary system, it is a form of alchemy: making money out of little or nothing. It has crossed the line from highly speculative to "probably being around and probably having some value in the future."

## The bull case

- Cannot be devalued by central bank printing; total supply is hard-coded at 21 million
- Growing need for gold-like storehold of wealth assets with limited supply, given massive debt and money creation
- Growing need for assets that can be privately held
- Easily portable and globally exchangeable
- Has stood the test of ~10 years without being hacked

## The bear case

**Supply is not truly limited.** Digital currencies as a category are unlimited because new competitors keep emerging. Better alternatives will eventually come along - that is how evolution works. If Blackberries were in limited supply they still would not be worth much because they were replaced by superior competitors.

**Cyber risk.** When the Department of Defense cannot protect its systems from hacking, it is naive to be fully comfortable that digital assets are safe. Cold storage exists but very few people actually use it.

**Government risk.** Governments have controlled money since the Bank of England in 1694. If Bitcoin becomes successful enough to threaten sovereign currencies, governments will try to kill it, and they have significant power to succeed. Bitcoin's biggest risk may be its own success.

**Privacy is overstated.** Bitcoin is a public ledger. A material amount is held non-privately. If governments (or hackers) want to see who holds what, privacy likely cannot be protected.

## Bridgewater's assessment

The Bridgewater research team concluded that Bitcoin shares some but not all qualities of a storehold of wealth. Three key challenges for institutional adoption:

1. **Extreme volatility** - future purchasing power remains fundamentally speculative
2. **Regulatory tail risk** - greater regulation might help institutional acceptance but could trigger selling by existing holders who value lack of oversight
3. **Liquidity constraints** - structural challenges for large institutional allocators

Bitcoin's diversification benefits are "more theoretical than realized." Its correlation with inflation expectations is inconsistent, and its ability to offset portfolio drawdowns has limited track record.

## Dalio's bottom line

Bitcoin looks like "a long-duration option on a highly unknown future that I could put an amount of money in that I wouldn't mind losing about 80% of."
