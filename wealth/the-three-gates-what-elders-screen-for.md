# The Three Gates: What Older/Wealthier People Screen For

![The three gates to the inner circle](https://assets.han-ws.workers.dev/i/2026/04/wealth-02-three-gates-inner-circle.svg)

Older/wealthier people run invisible tests on everyone they spend time with. These tests are drawn from traditions spanning 2,300+ years (Chanakya's testing protocol, Zeng Guofan's Bing Jian, Korean nunchi, Omi merchant graduated trust). The tests sort people into categories: "nice but no signal," "wants something," or "thinking independently, worth watching."

## Gate 1: Character test (passive observation)

Six things they watch without telling you:

### 1. Who you serve
Do you serve others before yourself? The person who takes the first portion without looking around reveals how they operate in resource-scarce situations.

### 2. How you treat staff
The waiter test. The single most reliable character indicator across traditions. The person warm to the host but dismissive to the server is showing two faces. Believe the face shown to the powerless.

### 3. Where your eyes go
Phone stays in your pocket the entire meal. Where attention goes when there is no agenda predicts where it goes when things get hard.

### 4. Under pressure
Self-control under alcohol or emotional pressure. Korean hoesik and Japanese nominication both use drinking as deliberate character assessment. If you get sloppy after two drinks, you've given them months of information in minutes.

### 5. Confidentiality
They share something mildly confidential and watch if it travels. Chanakya's 2,300-year-old test. This is pass/fail with no retake. If shared info appears elsewhere, you are permanently excluded from sensitive deal flow.

### 6. Ambiguity response
Given ambiguous instructions: do you ask clarifying questions (judgment), fill gaps with reasonable assumptions (initiative), freeze (dependence), or barrel ahead (arrogance)?

## Gate 2: Value test (active assessment)

### 1. Signal vs. presence
Do you bring useful information, or just show up? Bring a trend, a data point, a connection they haven't made.

### 2. Independent thought
Guiguzi's Quan technique: they say something slightly provocative. If you nod along, you stay on the periphery forever. If you offer a nuanced counter-opinion, you pass the independence test.

### 3. Useful introductions
When you connect people: generic "you two should meet" is worthless. Specific "you're both working on X" is powerful.

### 4. Consistent generosity
Share useful information same-day, no conditions. The math: consistent unconditional generosity creates a social norm others adopt in response.

### 5. Follow-through
The Mitsui protocol: small orders with specific requirements. One failure resets the relationship to zero. "Especially small ones" is the critical insight.

### 6. Honest calibration
Say "I don't know" freely. Rarer and more valuable than most people realize. They have been burned by polished people who performed expertise they didn't have.

## Gate 3: Role clarity (the mindset shift)

### Key mindset shift
You are there to extract judgment, not receive instructions. The Arab majlis model: absorb through exposure, don't obey.

### Questions that signal peer potential
- "What would you be watching for if you were evaluating this deal?"
- "What's the mistake you see younger operators make most often here?"
- "How did you think about risk when you were at my stage?"
- "I've been noticing [specific trend]. Does that match what you're seeing?"

### Questions that signal subordination (avoid)
- "What should I do about X?"
- "Can you introduce me to [person]?"
- "How do I get into [their industry]?"

### Handling "do this for me" moments
- Genuine capability test: do it excellently
- One-time busywork: do it gracefully, but don't let it become the pattern
- Consistent staff treatment: redirect by bringing something next time that only a peer could provide
